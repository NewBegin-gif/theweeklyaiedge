Emotion is every investor's worst enemy

In 23 years at international banks, I saw a lot. Smart, well-educated people with impressive résumés who, on paper, knew everything about risk management and diversification. And yet I watched them make the same mistake over and over: trading on emotion at the exact moment it mattered most.

Panic at a price dip, greed during a rally, fear of missing out when everyone else is buying in. The pattern repeats endlessly. Not because these people are stupid, but because human emotion is simply stronger than reason once prices move and your own money is on the line.

The cognitive dissonance of investing

The bizarre thing is that everyone knows it. Buy low, sell high — simple. But the moment the market drops 15 percent and the news is full of doom scenarios, the reptilian brain takes over. You feel physically uneasy. The urge to "do something" becomes overwhelming, even though you rationally know that selling at the bottom is often the worst possible move.

In a falling market, fear makes people sell at the bottom and miss the recovery that follows. Smart, experienced traders who threw their own plan overboard because the emotional pressure got too big. That's not weakness — that's biology.

Why a system makes the difference

The only way I know to break this mechanism is to work with a system that eliminates emotion. Fixed rules, set in advance, executed regardless of what the price does or how you feel. No interpretation, no "this time it's different," no midnight sessions checking your position.

That's why I built my own trading bot on Replit, connected to the API of Bitvavo. Not because I thought algorithms were magic, but because I knew that I was the problem. The bot has no opinion about the news, no fear during a dip, no FOMO during a rally. It executes the plan, period.

That's its only job, and that's exactly why it works. Every week I read the report the bot sends me — not to tinker, but to keep myself honest. The system runs, I watch, but I don't intervene.

The Edge Perspective

The hardest moment isn't during a crash — everyone knows emotion ruins things then. The hardest moment is when your system posts a loss three weeks in a row and you're certain you could do better. That's when you're tested. That's when you want to step in manually, tweak the rules, do something different "just this once." That's the moment you wreck everything. Discipline isn't exciting, but it's the only thing that works in the long run.

The trap of manual intervention

I've experienced this myself, multiple times. Weeks where the system gave a signal that went against my gut. The temptation to think: I have 23 years of experience, I can see the market is doing something different right now, let me just tweak the rule. And every time I did that, the result didn't improve — it got worse.

Not because my analysis was wrong, but because I was reasoning selectively. I only saw the data that confirmed my emotional impulse and ignored the rest. That's called confirmation bias. A system doesn't have that. It executes what's written, whether you sleep well over it or not.

That's why the real gain from automation isn't speed or efficiency — it's that you take yourself out of the equation. You build the system during a rational phase, test it thoroughly, and then let it run without intervening. That takes more discipline than most people think.

Time and peace of mind

The biggest difference for me personally isn't even financial — it's mental. I check the bot once a day, note where things stand, and get on with my life. No stress, no lying awake at night wondering whether I should do something, no constant urge to adjust positions.

I now spend that time with my family, building other projects, on things that actually matter. The tech does the grunt work. That was always the goal: getting time back by taking emotion out of the process.

Emotion isn't a character flaw. It's part of being human. But in investing, it's your worst enemy, and the only thing that helps is a system that decides for you when your reasoning fails you.
