AI automation for the one-person business: what actually works

When I left the banking world, I had no interest in hiring another team. I wanted time with my family in Saigon, not hours spent on schedules and meetings. Yet I now run six websites, a trading bot, and a content engine that publishes in 35 languages. How? By using AI agents and automation as if they were colleagues. No science fiction — just ordinary tools, used well.

This article is for solo business owners wondering whether AI automation could work for them too. I'll walk through what I built without a developer background, where I ran into trouble, and how to start sensibly — with one task you never want to do again.

Why I started automating

After 23 years in the corporate world, I knew one thing for certain: I didn't want to lose any more time to repetitive chores. Rewriting emails, translating content, updating dashboards, answering the same questions. That kind of work drains your energy and delivers little in return. So I started with one question: which task would I most like to hand off to a robot?

My answer: maintaining content for multiple markets. I run aibuildermarketplace.com, a B2B directory with reviews, and I didn't want to keep updating meta descriptions, translations, and content by hand every time. That's where Victor came in — my own AI agent running on a Hostinger VPS. He runs around the clock, keeps pages up to date, and generates programmatic SEO content in 35 languages. No fancy setup: a VPS, a script, and a clear brief.

The illusion of a team: what AI does, what I do

Today, as a one-person business, I simulate a company with 'multiple employees.' Victor handles content, my trading bot monitors the EUR/BTC market and trades according to fixed rules, and I focus on strategy and partnerships. Sounds smooth, but the reality is more sober.

What AI does do for me: carry out repetitive tasks (translating, pulling data, sending alerts), stay available around the clock, and make no mistakes as long as the instructions are right. What AI doesn't do: think creatively, maintain relationships, solve edge cases. That last category remains my job. I check the logs daily, step in when something goes wrong, and work out the strategy. The technology handles the chores, I handle the rest.

Where to start as a solo business owner

My advice: don't start with a vision of 'automating everything.' Start with one task you hate that's clearly defined. For example:

  • Categorizing and tagging emails (Zapier or Make can do this)
  • Generating daily summaries from RSS feeds (Claude or the ChatGPT API)
  • Scraping data and putting it into a spreadsheet (a Python script on a VPS)

I started with my trading bot: one simple rule (buy low, sell high within a range), connected to the Bitvavo API. No vision, no master plan. Just one thing automated well. Once that was running, I built Victor for content. Starting small keeps you from drowning in complexity.

A VPS like the one from Hostinger costs a few euros a month and gives you the room to run agents without depending on third parties. That's how I host Victor. No massive infrastructure required.

The Edge Perspective

I'm not a technical person, and yet I run systems that save me hours every week. The secret? I only automate what I've first done and understood by hand myself. No hype, no shotgun approach. One task at a time, well monitored, and I spend the time I win back with my family in Saigon. That's the edge: technology as a means, not an end.

What doesn't work: the pitfalls

AI automation sounds appealing, but there are catches. First: setup takes time. It took a lot of adjusting before Victor ran reliably and delivered the right output. Don't expect plug-and-play. Second: maintenance. Agents break when APIs change or when your input turns out unexpected. I check my logs daily.

And let's be honest: not everything should be automated. Customer contact, strategic decisions, creative work — there's little to gain there. I automate the chores, not the core work. That distinction is crucial. Get it wrong, and you build a house of cards that collapses the moment one link fails.

So: stay sober about it. Automate what's repetitive, time-consuming, and clearly defined. Do the rest yourself, or leave it be. That way, as a one-person business, you keep control and still have time left for what matters.
