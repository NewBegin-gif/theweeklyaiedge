How I Keep an Eye on a Bot That Trades While I Sleep

Building the bot was one thing. Trusting it to run unattended, day and night, turned out to be a different problem entirely. A bot that trades on my behalf without me watching every move is only useful if I can check, quickly and honestly, whether it is doing what I think it is doing. That is what monitoring is really about: not control, but visibility.

The dashboard I actually look at

My bot runs on Replit and talks to the Bitvavo exchange through its API, trading EUR against BTC. The dashboard I built shows me the current position, recent trades, and whether the bot is still connected to the exchange. I did not set out to build something fancy. I wanted something I could glance at from my phone in ten seconds and know if anything looked off. If you are thinking about building something similar, a platform like Replit is worth looking at, since it is what let me get a working dashboard running without a development background.

Telegram alerts for the moments that matter

A dashboard only helps if I remember to open it. So the bot also sends me Telegram messages when something specific happens: a trade is executed, the connection to the exchange drops, or an error occurs that stops the bot from running. These alerts are deliberately narrow. I did not want a stream of noise that I would eventually start ignoring. I would rather get three messages a week that each mean something than thirty that mean nothing.

The weekly report, and the lesson it taught me

On top of the dashboard and alerts, the bot generates a weekly summary: trades made, current holdings, basic activity over the past days. For a while I took this report at face value. It looked clean, it was formatted well, the numbers lined up with each other internally. That is exactly the problem. A report can be internally consistent and still be wrong, because it is only as accurate as the data feeding into it, and a small bug in how that data is pulled or calculated will not announce itself. It will just quietly produce a report that looks correct.

The only way I found to catch this is to occasionally stop trusting the report and check the raw numbers directly against the exchange itself. I log into Bitvavo, look at the actual trade history and balance, and compare that to what my dashboard and weekly report are telling me. Most of the time they match. When they have not, it was almost always a small issue in how the bot was logging or summarizing data, not an actual trading problem, but I would not have known that without checking the source directly.

The Edge Perspective

What I have learned from running this bot is that automation does not remove the need for attention, it changes what that attention looks like. I am not watching charts all day anymore, which was the point. But I have swapped that for something else: a habit of occasionally questioning the very tools that are supposed to tell me everything is fine. A dashboard is a summary, not a guarantee. The discipline is not in checking constantly, it is in checking the right thing occasionally, and being willing to believe the exchange over my own reporting when the two disagree.

None of this is about chasing better numbers. It is about knowing, with reasonable confidence, that the system doing the work while I am with my family is doing what I built it to do. If you want to experiment with something similar, Replit is where I started, though building the dashboard and alerts took time regardless of the platform. And whatever tools you use, keep checking the source data now and then. A report that looks right is not the same thing as a report that is right.
