What My AI Agent on a VPS Does All Day

People sometimes ask me what it actually means to run an AI agent. It sounds abstract until you describe the daily mechanics. I run an agent called Victor on a Hostinger VPS, and his job is to maintain my websites across 35 languages. That is not one big task, it is a long list of small, repeated ones. Here is what that actually looks like.

Scheduled jobs, not constant thinking

Victor does not sit there reasoning all day the way a person would. He runs on a schedule, like a series of cron jobs with an AI model behind them. At set times, a job wakes up, checks a list of pages, decides if anything needs building or updating, and does the work. Then it goes quiet until the next scheduled run. This is important to understand because it changes how you think about agents. They are not continuously alert. They act in bursts, on a timer, and the quality of what happens depends entirely on how well you have defined each job.

For my sites, the jobs are things like: check if a page exists for a given language and topic, check if the content is stale, check if a linked page still resolves, and rebuild or flag whatever fails those checks. Multiply that across 35 languages and a growing number of pages, and you get a workload that would be tedious for a person and is exactly the kind of repetitive thing I wanted off my plate.

A publishing chain with checks before anything goes live

The part I care most about is that nothing gets published blind. Victor's output goes through a chain of checks before it becomes a live page. That includes basic things like making sure the HTML is valid, that required fields are filled in, that the page is not a near-duplicate of something already published, and that it has not broken formatting rules I have set. Only after it clears those checks does it get published.

I built it this way because I do not trust any model, however capable, to self-certify its own output. The checks are separate steps, not the same process asking itself if it did a good job. That separation is the only thing standing between a glitch and something going live on one of my sites for weeks without me noticing.

Alerts when something fails

When a job fails a check, I get an alert. I do not watch a dashboard all day. I rely on the alert to tell me something needs attention, similar to how I use Telegram alerts for my trading bot. The alert does not fix anything, it just stops the bad output from going further and puts it in front of me. Most of the time the fix is small: a formatting rule that was too strict, a source page that moved, a language variant that needs a manual nudge. It is unglamorous work, but it is the work that keeps the system trustworthy.

The Edge Perspective

What strikes me most about running Victor is how quiet failure can be. An agent does not announce that it is repeating a mistake. It will happily do the wrong thing the exact same way, run after run, until a check is specific enough to catch it. That is the part I did not fully appreciate before I built this: the checks are not a formality, they are the actual safety net. Without them, you do not have an autonomous system, you have an unsupervised one, and those are not the same thing.

If you are weighing whether to run something like this yourself, I would start with the infrastructure question honestly answered, which is what I tried to do in my review of Hostinger, and then spend more time than feels necessary designing the checks before you let anything publish on its own.
